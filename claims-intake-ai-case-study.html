Teaching an AI agent to triage an insurance claim the moment it comes in

Every incoming claim needs a decision: does it need an adjuster right now, is the paperwork incomplete, or is it simple enough to fast-track? Claims Intake AI automates that first call — and treats a fatality claim completely differently from everything else.

The problem

First Notice of Loss (FNOL) intake — pulling the basic facts out of a claim and deciding who needs to look at it next — is one of the most manual, repetitive parts of the claims lifecycle. It's also explicitly not a coverage decision, which makes it a safely scoped task to automate: the system decides who looks at a claim next, never whether it's covered.

Carriers running AI-driven triage report claims resolved dramatically faster than fully manual review, with a meaningful share going fully touchless — proof this is a real efficiency lever the industry is already pulling, not a hypothetical.

How it works

A claim comes in with the basic facts. A deterministic engine checks it against a small number of explicit rules, in a specific order, and routes it to exactly one of three outcomes.

injury/fatality status, damage estimate, documentation status

Deliberate design decisions

Fatality is checked first, alone, before anything else

Every other rule in the engine can combine with others to build up a risk picture. Fatality doesn't — it short-circuits the whole function and escalates immediately at critical priority, on its own line of code, so it's impossible for a fatality claim to accidentally fall through a combination of other conditions.

The eval script reports fatality misses as their own number

A single "94% accuracy" headline could still hide a missed fatality claim inside the 6% error rate. The evaluation harness in this project deliberately breaks that number out on its own line, because it's the one metric a claims team cannot treat as acceptable background noise.

Ambiguous claims default to a human, not to automation

A claim that's too big for fast-track but doesn't trip any specific flag still escalates rather than auto-processing. The cost of an unnecessary adjuster look is small. The cost of a wrongly automated payout on a claim that needed scrutiny is not — so the system is deliberately conservative at that boundary.
